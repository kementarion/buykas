
# 1. Future Miner Revenue Issues

::: tip Problem Background

To keep the PoW system secure, miners need sufficient revenue.

Currently, more than three million Kaspa can be mined per day (October 2025), but 93% of the coins have already been mined.

In 2029, only about 300,000 can be mined per day.

In 2032, only about 30,000 can be mined per day.

Kaspa's design is to subsidize miners through transaction fees in the future, in order to keep the system secure. But transaction fees are currently too low—only a few thousand to tens of thousands of Kaspa per day.

- Transaction fee tracker: [https://www.kaspalytics.com/](https://www.kaspalytics.com/)

**Unless the price rises sharply, or transaction fees increase substantially…**

:::

### October 2025: Core Team Discussion

The core development team discussed transaction fees in the official group [https://t.me/kasparnd/4796](https://t.me/kasparnd/4796).

**Core Topic**: A proposal to dynamically set the network's minimum transaction fee rate (min fee threshold) through a miner voting mechanism, in order to address the blockchain network's security budget problem.

---

Summary of main points:

1. **Dynamic Fee Rate Proposal**
   - Proposed by Michael Sutton: miners vote to set the minimum transaction fee rate through extra data fields in blocks; all nodes on the network (including miners) tally the votes every 10 minutes and take the **25th percentile** of the votes as the current minimum fee rate standard.
   - **Purpose**: to avoid hard-coding the fee rate, letting miners adjust it dynamically according to market supply and demand, while assuming miners are cooperative and not greedy, so that consensus can be reached intelligently through the mechanism.

2. **Three-Stage Analysis of the System Security Budget** (explained by Yonatan Sompolinsky)
   - **Stage One (severe shortage of demand)**: real transaction demand is far below what the network security budget requires, and no mechanism (such as EIP-1559 or smart fee estimation) can solve the security budget problem. He believes Kaspa may currently be in this stage.
   - **Stage Two (sufficient demand but an imperfect mechanism)**: real demand is enough to cover the security budget, but existing mechanisms cannot incentivize users to reveal their true willingness to pay (they are not "incentive-compatible"). This stage needs a dynamic fee discovery mechanism (such as the proposal, or EIP-1559) to optimize.
   - **Stage Three (demand exceeds supply)**: the network is healthy and no complex mechanism is needed; ordinary bidding (such as "highest bidder wins") can meet demand.

3. **Basis for the 25th Percentile**
   - To prevent a single miner from accepting low-fee transactions for profit and triggering a "race to the bottom," the P2P network needs to follow the votes of the majority of miners (for example, 75%).
   - If the threshold were set at 90%, just 11% of miners could break coordination; 50% is safe but easily manipulated. A compromise at the 25th percentile (the standard supported by 75% of miners) balances protection against malicious price suppression with resistance to manipulation.

4. **Significance of the Discussion**
   - Even if we are currently in Stage One, exploring Stage Two mechanisms in advance helps with **market price discovery**, tests users' true willingness to pay, and prepares for a future transition.

---

Following the earlier discussion, here are three more distinct solutions proposed by Yonatan.

**Solution Three: Implement Useful Proof of Work**
*   **Core idea:** A completely independent solution is to introduce **useful proof of work** into the future roadmap.
*   **Path and conditions:**
    *   This is a **long-term solution**.
    *   We first need to observe and learn from the real results and successful experience of the **PoUW team**, then adapt them to the DAG (Directed Acyclic Graph) structure.
    *   Such a major change requires an **absolute majority consensus** in the community to implement.

**Solution Four: Create a New Token Standard (KRC-21)**
*   **Core idea:** Another distinct approach is to invent a new token standard—**KRC-21**—for **real DeFi tokens** (not meme coins) on L2 or built-in L2. Tokens designed under this standard would generate meaningful inflation (equivalent to a tax) and pay it to miners to support L1 security.
*   **Implementation and vision:**
    *   The standard **would not be enforced through the consensus protocol**, but it is believed it could form a strong **social consensus**.
    *   To better support Kaspa, such tokens should ideally be **required** to be exchanged for Kas first through public AMMs or CLOBs.
*   **Background inspiration:** This idea comes from Ethereum community discussions about how L2 can support L1 security and align interests.

**Solution Five (the most strategic core work): Advance Kaspa's Development Stage**
*   **Core idea:** The most important and most strategic work is to **help Kaspa move up from its current stage** (from Stage One to Stage Two, and then Stage Three).
*   **Status and outlook:**
    *   This follows Kaspa's established development trajectory.
    *   Many Kaspa community members (including the speaker himself) are working on this.
    *   More details are expected to be shared in the coming weeks.

#### (1). Article Translation

::: tip Translation:

Over the past few days, @hashdag (Yonatan) and I have been researching something. Let me explain briefly:

A 2017 paper by Lavi, Sattath, and Zohar proposed an alternative fee mechanism for blockchains. Its core idea is counterintuitive at first glance—**miners charge every transaction in their block the fee of the *lowest-paying* transaction included in that block** (even the transactions that agreed to pay more). The idea is to make miners optimize "number of transactions × lowest-value transaction," which naturally excludes transactions that pay far less than the others. You can (roughly) think of it as a smart, adaptive way of implementing a "minimum fee." The ideal effect of this mechanism is that it incentivizes users to pay miners higher fees in a way that is independent of block size (that is, even when the mempool is smaller than a single block's capacity).

First, it should be noted that, to my knowledge, this scheme has never been implemented in the real world.
Second, the above holds for blockchains. One of the challenges is how to apply it to DAGs. Before going on, I want to clear up a common misconception about Kaspa:

---------------**Interlude: Inclusive Blockchains**---------------

Kaspa's high throughput comes primarily *not* from transactions in parallel blocks—even if the transactions on chains that are not selected were discarded, **Kaspa would still have more capacity than a traditional blockchain simply because it allows a higher block rate for a given block size** (…for given security parameters and minimum hardware requirements, etc.). But it is true that as the number of parallel blocks grows, this simple effect weakens—if you discard the transactions in parallel blocks, that throughput is wasted. It can be recovered with a scheme called an "inclusive protocol." In short, it assigns an order to the parallel blocks, and every transaction that does not conflict with earlier ones is recorded in the ledger as usual. *In theory*, we can now let every block achieve its maximum throughput.

But why don't all parallel blocks include the same transactions, which would waste our theoretical gains? Well, if they all picked the same transactions, sometimes they would do very well, but more often they would get nothing. So a miner who picks transactions that other miners did not pick will, on average, do better! Even if those transactions pay lower fees. Long story short, it turns out that the "correct" (the "equilibrium," with about five asterisks of caveats) thing for them to do is to pick transactions at random, but **weighted by the fees those transactions pay**. This is heavily simplified, for the record. Coincidentally, this also tends to give the system good throughput. That is the current situation with Kaspa, but again, **this is not the only source of Kaspa's high throughput**.

---------------------**End of Interlude**---------------------

So basically, although in theory we could step back and give up "inclusiveness" (sacrificing some valuable throughput), we want to combine this new fee mechanism with the insights of the inclusive protocol Kaspa currently uses.

After adopting the new fee mechanism, the equilibrium changes; as miners adopt different strategies, the original guarantees about user behavior also need to be re-examined—so once the miners' equilibrium is established, we also need to work out the users' equilibrium.

We hope that, after all the analysis is done, we will find either that we can maintain user-behavior guarantees similar to those in the original paper, or that we can find a variant of the original scheme that achieves this.

By the way, if anyone with a relevant background wants to work on this problem too, we would very much welcome it. As with everything else, long-term decentralization of research is also a goal.

Two final points:

- a) Any change to the fee mechanism, however good, **cannot replace the importance of growing demand**. This effort should proceed in parallel with building new utility for Kaspa (for example, vProgs).

- b) This is a **medium-term solution** (but I think it is serious research that is long overdue), and it requires a hard fork. Some changes that could be implemented immediately have been proposed within the community. They may lack a solid theoretical basis in the long run, but are usually harmless—for example, a miner-adjustable minimum fee. Such solutions tend to fall into a prisoner's dilemma, and as I said, I don't think fee policy alone can change the big picture right now—but if someone wants to implement it, I personally would not object, because it is harmless. To me, it sounds like a good entry-level project for getting into Kaspa development in Rust.

:::

#### (2). Comparison with Kaspa's Current Fee Mechanism

Compared with Kaspa's current mechanism, the core advantage of the paper's mechanism is: **it can intelligently and adaptively establish and maintain a "market minimum fee" standard in a decentralized system, potentially raising miners' overall revenue and improving the network's security budget.**

1.  **Create a "Fee Floor" to Resist the "Tragedy of the Commons"**
    *   **Problem with the current mechanism**: When block space is plentiful, miners have a strong incentive to include any transaction with a fee greater than zero, because something is better than nothing. This easily drives fee competition toward zero, forming a "prisoner's dilemma" that weakens network security.
    *   **How the paper's mechanism solves it**: It changes miners' incentives. Miners no longer simply try to fill blocks, but try to **maximize (block capacity × minimum fee in the block)**. This incentivizes miners to ignore transactions that bid too low, naturally forming a "fee floor" in the market.

2.  **Incentivize Users to Pay Higher Fees**
    *   **Current mechanism**: A user who just wants to be included in a block, with no need for acceleration, has a strong incentive to pay an extremely low fee.
    *   **Paper's mechanism**: If the fee a user pays is below the current market "floor price," their transaction is ignored. To be included, they must pay at least a fee equivalent to the current "floor price." This directly pulls users into the system that maintains the network's fee level.

3.  **May Suit a Future High-Throughput, Low-Congestion State Better**
    *   If Kaspa succeeds in reaching its high-throughput goals, the network may be **uncongested** most of the time. Under the current mechanism, that easily drives fees toward zero. The paper's mechanism is precisely a solution for this "post-success scenario," **maintaining a meaningful fee market even when block space is not scarce** and providing sustained support for network security (the security budget).

---

Although the paper's mechanism has theoretical advantages, Kaspa's current mechanism is also strongly viable because of its simplicity and efficiency.

1.  **Simple, Effective, and Easy to Implement**
    *   The current mechanism is very intuitive, does not require changing users' behavior (users still bid on the "highest bidder wins" intuition), and is easier to engineer in a complex DAG structure.

2.  **Efficient Resource Allocation Under High Demand**
    *   When transaction demand is strong and the mempool is full, the current mechanism efficiently allocates block space to the transactions with the highest willingness to pay, which is itself efficient market behavior.

3.  **Good Synergy with the "Inclusive Protocol"**
    *   As the article points out, the current fee-weighted random selection strategy happens to encourage miners to choose different transactions, perfectly leveraging the DAG's inclusive protocol and recovering the potential throughput loss caused by parallel blocks. This is an "unintentional but very beautiful" fit. Changing the fee mechanism might break this delicate balance.
